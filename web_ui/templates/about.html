How any of this works

No jargon, nothing assumed. If you have ever wondered what these things actually are, this is the whole picture in about ten minutes.

What this app is

You describe something you want — a game, a website, a story, a tool to sort out your photos — and an AI builds it on this computer while you watch. You never have to type code, name a file, or open anything technical. When it is done, you press Play and it runs.

The app does not come with its own AI. It connects to one you have an account with, using a key you paste into Settings once. That means you pay the AI company directly, at their prices, with nothing added on top. It also means you can change your mind and use a different one whenever you like.

What the AI actually does

It reads text, and it writes text. That is the whole of it.

Everything else you have heard about these things is built on top of that one ability. It has no body, no eyes on your room, no opinions it is trying to spread, and nothing it wants when nobody is talking to it. Between your messages it is not waiting, or thinking, or planning. It is not doing anything at all.

What it knows, it learned once, in one enormous go, from an enormous amount of writing. That took months and cost millions of dollars. At the end of it the knowledge was set, the way a fired pot is set — and then it stopped learning. Talking to it does not teach it anything. Nothing you say here goes back into it. Tomorrow it will know exactly what it knows today, and not a word more.

Two things follow from that, and both matter more than they sound.

  • Its knowledge is old. Everything it learned stopped at some point months or years ago. It often does not realise this, and will tell you confidently about a tool that has changed since. If something depends on being current, tell it to look it up on the web first — it can, and it will, but it frequently does not think to.
  • It is good at what it saw a lot of and bad at the rest. Common things it handles beautifully. Something genuinely unusual, it will attempt with exactly the same confidence and get wrong.

Why it forgets, and what happens when it does

The closest thing in real life is a person who has lost the ability to form new memories. Everything they knew before the injury is intact — their family, their skills, that the sky is blue — and none of it is going anywhere. But nothing that happens today sticks. Tomorrow, it is gone.

People in that situation cope with notes. Everything that matters gets written down and kept where they can see it, and they read the whole stack again each morning before they can carry on. That stack is the only thing standing between them and starting from nothing.

Your conversation is the stack of notes. Everything you have said, everything it has said, every file it has opened and every command it has run — the AI re-reads all of it, from the top, before writing each single reply. Not a summary of it. The actual thing, every time. That is not a way of describing how it works. It is how it works.

And there is only so much it can hold. When the stack gets close to that limit, this app does something you will occasionally see happen: it writes a summary of the older part and clears that part away. What it did, what it decided, which files it changed, what is still to do — kept. The exact back-and-forth of two hours ago — gone, replaced by a paragraph about it.

You will see “Earlier conversation was summarized” in the transcript when this happens. Nothing has broken. It is the app making room, and it is why a project can run all day.

It is worth knowing because of what it means for you: if something matters and you mentioned it a long way back, say it again — it may be reading a summary of that part rather than your actual words. And if you are about to start something quite different, a fresh project is cheaper and sharper than carrying an old conversation into it.

What it can do to your computer

On its own, an AI can only produce text. It cannot open a file or run anything. What changes that is an app like this one, which reads what it wrote and acts on it — makes the file it described, runs the command it asked for. That is where the power comes from. Not from the AI. From us handing it the keys.

So it is worth being straight with you about which keys we hand over.

It does not stop to ask permission

Most tools like this interrupt you constantly: may I read this? may I run that? This one does not, for work inside your own project. It gets on with it. That is deliberate — this app is for people who would have no way of judging those questions, and who in practice would press “yes” to all of them anyway, which is a worse kind of safe. Asking about a file you are working on forty times a day is how a person learns to stop reading the question.

There is exactly one thing it does stop and ask about, and it is the next section.

What replaces the asking is a small number of things it simply cannot do, whatever it decides: it cannot run the handful of commands that wipe a machine, and it cannot write to the parts of the system that would break it. Those refusals are absolute and it is told to explain them to you rather than work around them.

Files outside your project need your say-so

Each project lives in its own folder, and inside that folder the AI works freely — that is its work and you are not asked about it. The moment it reaches for a file outside that folder, it stops and asks you, naming the file. You can allow that one file, allow everything in that folder from then on, or say no. Saying no is not a problem: it carries on and tells you what it could not do.

Child mode works the same way, with one difference: only a grown-up can say yes. Both of the “yes” buttons are labeled needs a grown-up and ask for the parent password. Saying no never does — a child should always be able to stop something without needing permission first.

A few places never ask, because a question there would be noise and noise is what teaches people to click past these boxes: the computer's temporary folder, anything you have just attached to the chat, and your own other projects.

Where that line does not reach. This covers the way the AI reads and writes files, which is how it handles files essentially all of the time. It does not cover every shell command: installing software genuinely does touch the rest of the computer, and an app that tried to police each command would either block real work or make promises it could not keep. We would rather tell you exactly where the line falls than draw you a bigger one that is not there.

When it is wrong — and it will be

It writes the wrong answer in exactly the same voice as the right one. There is no wobble, no hedge, nothing in the tone to warn you. This is the single most useful thing to understand about these things, and it is the one people learn last.

The good news is that in this app, being wrong is usually loud. Code that does not work does not work in front of you: you press Play and the button does nothing. That is a far better place to be wrong than an essay, where nobody can tell.

When it tells you something that actually matters — that a thing is safe, that something costs what it says, that a method is the right one — do not take it on trust. Ask it to prove it. “How could we check that?” is the most valuable sentence in this whole app. It will usually come up with a way, and sometimes you will have to help it invent one. That habit is worth more than any amount of caution.

The honest bad parts

It uses real energy and real water. Every reply is worked out in a building full of machines that need power and cooling. One reply is small. Hundreds of millions of replies a day are not, and the data centers behind them are being built at a pace that has real consequences for real places. Using it thoughtfully rather than idly is not nothing.

It can be used to do harm. Anything that writes convincingly can write convincing lies, at a scale and a cheapness that did not exist before. That is happening and it is not a hypothetical.

It reflects what it was trained on. It learned from an enormous amount of human writing, and human writing contains every one of our habits, good and bad. When one of these behaves badly it is usually showing us something about us. That is an explanation, not an excuse.

The research is young and argued over. You will read confident headlines in both directions — that these things are dangerous, that they are useless, that they make people cleverer, that they make people stupider. Some of that work is careful and some of it is not, and the field is too new to have settled. Treat strong claims about AI the way this page has just told you to treat strong claims from AI.

Should you be frightened of it?

Not of the kind you see in movies — the machine that wakes up, decides things for itself and turns on us. That is a genuinely different kind of thing from what anyone has actually built, and the difference is not a technicality.

What we have built cannot learn while it runs. It was trained once and then frozen. It does not act unless something asks it to, and when the answer is finished it stops. It has no ongoing existence in between. Every serious version of the takeover story needs a system that keeps learning, keeps running, and wants something. Nothing in this app is any of those.

The real risk is duller and much closer to home: an AI does whatever it is told, and it is bad at working out who is telling it. Hide an instruction inside a web page, and an AI that reads that page may follow it as though it came from you. That is called prompt injection. Nobody has solved it, and it is why this app keeps a child away from the open web.

So the danger is not a machine deciding to do something terrible. It is a person pointing an obedient machine at something terrible — or hiding a note where it will read one. Both of those are people problems, and people problems are the kind we have always had.

Thoughtful people do disagree about the long run, and this page is not going to pretend that argument is over. But the near danger is misuse and mistakes, not machines with plans.

Why we think it is worth it

Until very recently, making software cost years twice over. Years to learn enough to start — and then years again to actually build the thing, because even for someone who knew exactly what they were doing, a real website or a real app is thousands of small decisions and tens of thousands of lines. Most people with a good idea never found out whether it was any good. They gave up somewhere in the middle, or shipped something half-finished, or decided this sort of thing was not for them.

That barrier is gone. A ten-year-old with an idea can have it running this afternoon. A parent with a small business can have the tool they have wanted for five years. Whatever else is true about all of this, that part is an enormous, straightforwardly good thing, and it is what this app exists to hand to your family.

This app was built this way too. Every line of it was written by an AI, working from plain-English descriptions of what it should do and why — the same thing your child will be doing on the other side of this screen. It is not a demonstration of what the technique might one day manage. It is the technique, running.

It also gives you back the part of the day nobody misses. You say what you want, and while it works you can get up, go outside, sit with someone, eat. You come back, look at what it did, and say what to change. That is a better shape for a life than sitting still and typing for eight hours, and it is available now.

AI lets us be more human.

Not because it does the thinking — because it takes the part that was never really thinking. The boilerplate, the fiddly syntax, the two-day hunt for a missing semicolon. What is left is the part that was always the point: deciding what to make, judging whether it is any good, and caring how it turns out.

When you outgrow this

You may, eventually, and it would be a strange kind of honesty to pretend otherwise. This app is built for someone who has never done any of this: it does one project at a time, it explains itself as it goes, and it connects to an AI you pay per use — which is the right arrangement while you are finding out whether you like it, because a quiet week costs nothing.

If your child ends up building constantly, that arrangement stops being the cheap one. At that point the thing to look at is Claude Code, which is what this app was built with. It is a professional tool with a professional learning curve — a terminal, and no hand-holding — so it is not where anyone should start. But a flat monthly subscription buys far more work than the same money spent per use, and it runs the strongest models there are.

We would rather say that than keep somebody here who has outgrown us. The point of this app is not to hold your family; it is to get them started, and started well.

Two futures, and the difference is how it is used

One is a world where people stop bothering. The machine does it, so nobody learns to read closely or think a problem through, and a generation grows up unable to check anything. That future is available and it is not far-fetched.

The other is a world where the same tool is used to climb: where a child learns because they wanted to build something and had to understand it to finish, where being stuck is interesting instead of humiliating, and where a person can go as far as their curiosity carries them rather than as far as their schooling let them.

The difference is entirely in how it is used. That is why this app keeps explaining what it is doing instead of just doing it, why it is told never to hand a child a homework answer, and why you are reading this page. We are trying very hard to build the second one.

Still unsure about something? Ask the assistant on the home page. It can explain any of this in as much or as little detail as you like, and it will not mind being asked twice.

How any of this works

No jargon, nothing assumed. If you have ever wondered what these things actually are, this is the whole picture in about ten minutes.

What this app is

You describe something you want — a game, a website, a story, a tool to sort out your photos — and an AI builds it on this computer while you watch. You never have to type code, name a file, or open anything technical. When it is done, you press Play and it runs.

The app does not come with its own AI. It connects to one you have an account with, using a key you paste into Settings once. That means you pay the AI company directly, at their prices, with nothing added on top. It also means you can change your mind and use a different one whenever you like.

What the AI actually does

It reads text, and it writes text. That is the whole of it.

Everything else you have heard about these things is built on top of that one ability. It has no body, no eyes on your room, no opinions it is trying to spread, and nothing it wants when nobody is talking to it. Between your messages it is not waiting, or thinking, or planning. It is not doing anything at all.

What it knows, it learned once, in one enormous go, from an enormous amount of writing. That took months and cost millions of pounds. At the end of it the knowledge was set, the way a fired pot is set — and then it stopped learning. Talking to it does not teach it anything. Nothing you say here goes back into it. Tomorrow it will know exactly what it knows today, and not a word more.

Two things follow from that, and both matter more than they sound.

  • Its knowledge is old. Everything it learned stopped at some point months or years ago. It often does not realise this, and will tell you confidently about a tool that has changed since. If something depends on being current, tell it to look it up on the web first — it can, and it will, but it frequently does not think to.
  • It is good at what it saw a lot of and bad at the rest. Common things it handles beautifully. Something genuinely unusual, it will attempt with exactly the same confidence and get wrong.

Why it forgets, and what happens when it does

Imagine someone who has read every book in the library and remembers all of it — but can only hold a few pages on the desk in front of them at a time. The library is what they learned during training. The desk is your conversation.

Everything you have said, everything it has said, every file it has read and every command it has run this session — all of that has to sit on the desk, all at once, every single time it answers you. That is not a metaphor for how it works. It is how it works: it re-reads the entire conversation from the beginning before writing each reply.

The desk has an edge. When the conversation gets close to it, this app does something you will occasionally see happen: it writes a summary of the older part and clears that part away. What it did, what it decided, which files it changed, what is still to do — kept. The exact back-and-forth of two hours ago — gone, replaced by a paragraph about it.

You will see “Earlier conversation was summarized” in the transcript when this happens. Nothing has broken. It is the app making room, and it is why a project can run all day.

It is worth knowing because of what it means for you: if something matters and you mentioned it a long way back, say it again. And if you are about to start something quite different, a fresh project is cheaper and sharper than carrying an old conversation into it.

What it can do to your computer

On its own, an AI can only produce text. It cannot open a file or run anything. What changes that is an app like this one, which reads what it wrote and acts on it — makes the file it described, runs the command it asked for. That is where the power comes from. Not from the AI. From us handing it the keys.

So it is worth being straight with you about which keys we hand over.

It does not stop to ask permission

Most tools like this interrupt you constantly: may I read this? may I run that? This one does not. It gets on with the work. That is a deliberate choice — this app is for people who would have no way of judging those questions, and who in practice would press “yes” to all of them anyway, which is a worse kind of safe.

What replaces the asking is a small number of things it simply cannot do, whatever it decides: it cannot run the handful of commands that wipe a machine, and it cannot write to the parts of the system that would break it. Those refusals are absolute and it is told to explain them to you rather than work around them.

It works in your project folder — but it is not fenced into it

Each project lives in its own folder, and that is where the AI works. It is not locked in there, though. If it needs to install something or look at a setting elsewhere on the machine, it can, exactly as any program you run can. It cannot reach anything your own account cannot.

In practice it is uninterested in your files. It looks at what is in its project, and it opens things because a job needs them opened, not to have a look around. But we would rather say plainly what is possible than tell you it is fenced in when it is not.

If that matters to you, the simplest answer is the ordinary one for any program: keep the genuinely private things in a separate account on the computer, or off it.

When it is wrong — and it will be

It writes the wrong answer in exactly the same voice as the right one. There is no wobble, no hedge, nothing in the tone to warn you. This is the single most useful thing to understand about these things, and it is the one people learn last.

The good news is that in this app, being wrong is usually loud. Code that does not work does not work in front of you: you press Play and the button does nothing. That is a far better place to be wrong than an essay, where nobody can tell.

When it tells you something that actually matters — that a thing is safe, that something costs what it says, that a method is the right one — do not take it on trust. Ask it to prove it. “How could we check that?” is the most valuable sentence in this whole app. It will usually come up with a way, and sometimes you will have to help it invent one. That habit is worth more than any amount of caution.

The honest bad parts

It uses real energy and real water. Every reply is worked out in a building full of machines that need power and cooling. One reply is small. Hundreds of millions of replies a day are not, and the data centres behind them are being built at a pace that has real consequences for real places. Using it thoughtfully rather than idly is not nothing.

It can be used to do harm. Anything that writes convincingly can write convincing lies, at a scale and a cheapness that did not exist before. That is happening and it is not a hypothetical.

It reflects what it was trained on. It learned from an enormous amount of human writing, and human writing contains every one of our habits, good and bad. When one of these behaves badly it is usually showing us something about us. That is an explanation, not an excuse.

The research is young and argued over. You will read confident headlines in both directions — that these things are dangerous, that they are useless, that they make people cleverer, that they make people stupider. Some of that work is careful and some of it is not, and the field is too new to have settled. Treat strong claims about AI the way this page has just told you to treat strong claims from AI.

Should you be frightened of it?

Not of the film version, no. The machine that wakes up, decides things on its own and turns on us is a different kind of thing from what we have actually built, and the difference is not a detail.

What we have built cannot learn while it runs. It was trained once and then frozen. It does not act unless something asks it to, and when the answer is finished it stops. It has no ongoing existence in between. Every serious version of the takeover story needs a system that keeps learning, keeps running, and wants something. Nothing in this app is any of those.

Where the real risk sits is duller and closer: it is about who gets to tell one what to do. These things follow instructions, and they are not very good at telling whose instructions they are. Hide a message in a web page, and an AI reading that page may treat it as an order from you. That is called prompt injection, nobody has solved it, and it is the reason this app keeps a child's browsing away from the open web.

So the thing to watch is not an AI deciding to do something dreadful. It is a person aiming a capable, obedient system at something dreadful — or slipping a note into its reading. That is a problem about people, which is the sort of problem we have always had.

Thoughtful people do disagree about the long run, and this page is not going to pretend that argument is over. But the near danger is misuse and mistakes, not machines with plans.

Why we think it is worth it

Until very recently, making software meant years of learning before you could build the thing you actually wanted. Most people who had a good idea simply never got to find out whether it was any good. The barrier was never imagination. It was punctuation.

That barrier has gone. A ten-year-old with an idea can have it running this afternoon. A parent with a small business can have the tool they have wanted for five years. Whatever else is true about all this, that part is an enormous, straightforwardly good thing, and it is what this app exists to hand to your family.

It also gives you back the part of the day nobody misses. You say what you want, and while it works you can get up, go outside, sit with someone, eat. You come back, look at what it did, and say what to change. That is a better shape for a life than sitting still and typing for eight hours, and it is available now.

AI lets us be more human.

Not because it does the thinking — because it takes the part that was never really thinking. The boilerplate, the fiddly syntax, the two-day hunt for a missing semicolon. What is left is the part that was always the point: deciding what to make, judging whether it is any good, and caring how it turns out.

Two futures, and the difference is how it is used

One is a world where people stop bothering. The machine does it, so nobody learns to read closely or think a problem through, and a generation grows up unable to check anything. That future is available and it is not far-fetched.

The other is a world where the same tool is used to climb: where a child learns because they wanted to build something and had to understand it to finish, where being stuck is interesting instead of humiliating, and where a person can go as far as their curiosity carries them rather than as far as their schooling let them.

The difference is entirely in how it is used. That is why this app keeps explaining what it is doing instead of just doing it, why it is told never to hand a child a homework answer, and why you are reading this page. We are trying very hard to build the second one.

Still unsure about something? Ask the assistant on the home page. It can explain any of this in as much or as little detail as you like, and it will not mind being asked twice.

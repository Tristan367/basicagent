What it does

You describe something — a game, a website, a story, a tool to sort out your photos — and it makes it, explaining what it is doing as it goes. When it is finished you press Play and the thing runs. If something is wrong you say so in plain English and it fixes it.

It is built for people who have never done any of this: a parent, a teacher, a child, somebody who has an idea and no way to build it. It works by voice if you would rather talk than type, and it reads its replies out loud if you would rather listen than read.

Setting it up

Once, and then never again. Download the file above and unzip it. Inside are three files named Install on Windows, Install on Mac and Install on Linux — open the one for your computer, and there is a Read me first beside them saying the same. When it finishes you will have an Assistant icon on your desktop — that is what you click from then on. You do not need the installer, the folder, or this page ever again.

It does not matter where you unzip it. The installer copies the app somewhere permanent for you and tells you where it went, so leaving the download in your Downloads folder is fine — you can delete that folder afterwards. This matters more than it sounds: Windows can be set to clear out Downloads every month, and an app installed there would go with it.

Windows

  1. Right-click the downloaded file, choose Extract All, and open the folder it makes.
  2. Double-click Install on Windows and wait. Windows will say it does not recognise who made this — choose More info, then Run anyway. (Why it says that.)
  3. It puts an Assistant icon on your desktop and in the Start menu. Double-click that whenever you want it.

This app is built on Python. If your computer has not got it, the installer offers to fetch it for you from python.org and checks the download before running it — you press one key and it handles the rest. It never needs an administrator.

Mac

  1. Double-click the downloaded file, and open the folder it makes.
  2. Open Install on Mac. The first time, macOS will refuse because it cannot tell who made it: right-click the file, choose Open, then Open again. (Why it does that.)
  3. If this Mac has not got a recent enough Python, it offers to fetch one from python.org and hands it to Apple's own installer. That step asks for your password, as anything installing software does.
  4. It puts an Assistant app in your Applications folder and on your desktop. Open that whenever you want it — you can drag it to the Dock like anything else.

Linux

  1. unzip -d Assistant Assistant-Setup.zip && cd Assistant
  2. python3 app/install.py
  3. It adds Assistant to your applications menu. Or run basicagent.

Is it safe?

Your computer is going to warn you about this, and it is better to hear why now than to meet the warning cold. Windows and Mac put a notice in front of anything whose publisher they cannot identify, and identifying yourself means buying a certificate — a few hundred dollars a year, forever. I have not bought one. So the warning says the software comes from an unknown publisher, which is exactly true: it comes from me.

That is a warning worth taking seriously in general, so here is what I can offer in place of having paid for it to go away.

You can look

Every line of it is public and written to be read — here. Nothing is hidden or compiled. If you know someone who writes software, this is a reasonable thing to ask them to glance at.

The download is built from that code, by GitHub

Not on my computer, where I could have put anything in it. GitHub builds each release from the public code and signs a statement saying so, attached to the download. Someone technical can check that with one command — it is written on the release page. That is the part that turns "the code you can read is the file you are getting" from something you take on trust into something anybody can verify.

It stays in its own corner

It never asks for administrator or root. It installs into your own user folder, and there is an Uninstall beside it — on Windows it is in Settings under Apps like anything else — which removes the program, the icons and the browser it downloaded, and asks separately before touching anything you have made. It talks to three places and nowhere else: the AI company whose account you connect; GitHub, to check once a day for a new version and to fetch the voices during setup; and DuckDuckGo, when you ask it to look something up. There is no account with me, no sign-up, and no analytics — I do not find out that you are using it.

The honest part

The real risk here was never that this is a virus. It is an assistant that writes programs and runs them on your computer, because that is the entire point of it — so it can also make a mess, change a file it should have left alone, or be led somewhere daft by a web page it was asked to read. It asks permission before touching anything outside the project it is working on. The page inside the app called How any of this works goes through all of this plainly, including the parts that are not reassuring, and it is worth ten minutes before you hand this to a child.

Who made this, and why

Tristan Johnson. It started as one idea: you should not have to be technical to use this. The tools that write software for you are genuinely astonishing now, and almost all of them are still aimed at people who already know what a terminal is. That is backwards. An ordinary person ought to be able to describe a game, a website, a tool for sorting out their photos — and get it.

Then it kept going. Once it worked by voice and read itself out loud, it turned out to work for somebody who cannot see the screen, and for somebody who cannot use a mouse, and for somebody who is eight, and for somebody who is eighty. Those were not separate features; they were the same feature finished properly.

And the moment it could do that, it was obviously a homeschooling tool. So there is a child mode, a way for a parent to say in their own words how it should talk to their child, and a project manager that handles the technical decisions so nobody in the family has to. Both my parents teach. The test I actually use is whether my mother could sit down with it and build something without asking me a single question.

There is no company here and nothing to sign up for. My name is on it on purpose: if it turns out to be a nuisance on your computer, you know exactly whose fault that is and where to find me. Tell me here, and I will fix it.

What it costs

The app is free and always will be. It has no AI of its own — it connects to one you have an account with, which is why it can be given away and why it costs so little to run.

Start with Google. Sign in, press one button, paste the code it gives you. No card, nothing to pay, a few hundred goes a day — enough to build something real. The app walks you through it with a picture of every click.

For parents

There is a child mode. It makes the assistant patient and age-appropriate, never hands over homework answers, keeps the browsing off the open web, and locks the settings behind a password. There is also a box where you write, in your own words, how you want it to speak to your child — what to encourage, what to leave alone, what your family believes. It follows that closely, and your child cannot see it.

There is a page inside the app called How any of this works that explains what these things actually are, what they get wrong, and the honest bad parts. It is worth ten minutes before you hand this to anybody.

Updating

It checks once a day and tells you when there is a new version, with a button that does the whole thing. Your projects, settings and keys are kept somewhere separate and are never touched by an update.

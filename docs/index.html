Assistant

Say what you want, and it builds it on your computer while you watch. No code, no jargon, nothing technical to learn first.

Free. Windows, Mac and Linux. Takes about five minutes to set up, once. Is it safe?

What it does

You describe something — a game, a website, a story, a tool to sort out your photos — and it makes it, explaining what it is doing as it goes. When it is finished you press Play and the thing runs. If something is wrong you say so in plain English and it fixes it.

It is built for people who have never done any of this: a parent, a teacher, a child, somebody who has an idea and no way to build it. It works by voice if you would rather talk than type, and it reads its replies out loud if you would rather listen than read.

Setting it up

Once, and then never again. Download the file above and unzip it, then run the installer below. When it finishes you will have an Assistant icon on your desktop — that is what you click from then on. You do not need the installer, the folder, or this page ever again.

Windows

  1. Open the folder you unzipped.
  2. Double-click install.bat and wait. Windows will say it does not recognise who made this — choose More info, then Run anyway. (Why it says that.)
  3. It puts an Assistant icon on your desktop and in the Start menu. Double-click that whenever you want it.

If it says Python is missing, it will tell you where to get it. Install that, then run install.bat again.

Mac

  1. Open the folder you unzipped.
  2. Double-click install.command. The first time, macOS will refuse because it cannot tell who made it: right-click the file, choose Open, then Open again. (Why it does that.)
  3. It puts an Assistant app in your Applications folder and on your desktop. Open that whenever you want it — you can drag it to the Dock like anything else.

Linux

  1. Open a terminal in the folder you unzipped.
  2. python3 install.py
  3. It adds Assistant to your applications menu. Or run python3 basicagent.py.

Is it safe?

Your computer is going to warn you about this, and it is better to hear why now than to meet the warning cold. Windows and Mac put a notice in front of anything whose publisher they cannot identify, and identifying yourself means buying a certificate — a few hundred dollars a year, forever. I have not bought one. So the warning says the software comes from an unknown publisher, which is exactly true: it comes from me.

That is a warning worth taking seriously in general, so here is what I can offer in place of having paid for it to go away.

You can look

Every line of it is public and written to be read — here. Nothing is hidden or compiled. If you know someone who writes software, this is a reasonable thing to ask them to glance at.

The download is built from that code, by GitHub

Not on my computer, where I could have put anything in it. GitHub builds each release from the public code and signs a statement saying so, attached to the download. Someone technical can check that with one command — it is written on the release page. That is the part that turns "the code you can read is the file you are getting" from something you take on trust into something anybody can verify.

It stays in its own corner

It never asks for administrator or root. It installs into your own user folder, and uninstalling is deleting that folder. It talks to three places and nowhere else: the AI company whose account you connect, GitHub once a day to see whether there is a newer version, and DuckDuckGo when you ask it to look something up. There is no account with me, no sign-up, and no analytics — I do not find out that you are using it.

The honest part

The real risk here was never that this is a virus. It is an assistant that writes programs and runs them on your computer, because that is the entire point of it — so it can also make a mess, change a file it should have left alone, or be led somewhere daft by a web page it was asked to read. It asks permission before touching anything outside the project it is working on. The page inside the app called How any of this works goes through all of this plainly, including the parts that are not reassuring, and it is worth ten minutes before you hand this to a child.

What it costs

The app is free and always will be. It has no AI of its own — it connects to one you have an account with, which is why it can be given away and why it costs so little to run.

Start with Google. Sign in, press one button, paste the code it gives you. No card, nothing to pay, a few hundred goes a day — enough to build something real. The app walks you through it with a picture of every click.

For parents

There is a child mode. It makes the assistant patient and age-appropriate, never hands over homework answers, keeps the browsing off the open web, and locks the settings behind a password. There is also a box where you write, in your own words, how you want it to speak to your child — what to encourage, what to leave alone, what your family believes. It follows that closely, and your child cannot see it.

There is a page inside the app called How any of this works that explains what these things actually are, what they get wrong, and the honest bad parts. It is worth ten minutes before you hand this to anybody.

Updating

It checks once a day and tells you when there is a new version, with a button that does the whole thing. Your projects, settings and keys are kept somewhere separate and are never touched by an update.
